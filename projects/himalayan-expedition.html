Case study 01 · Interactive analysis

Himalayan Expedition Risk Analysis

I turned 75 years of expedition records into a dashboard that helps people compare historical risk, success, team size, season, and altitude—without losing sight of the denominator behind each number.

01 · Overview

Making a large historical record explorable

A raw death count can make one season or team type look dangerous simply because more expeditions took place there. I built this project to keep exposure visible, let people move between multiple views of the record, and make comparisons with the right unit.

Dataset at a glance

11,358 expedition records across 414 peaks

The analysis spans 1950–2024. In the prepared dataset, 55.1% of expeditions recorded main-peak success. That is an expedition-level share, not an individual climber’s probability of summiting.

Questions I designed around

01

How does the number of member deaths per 100 expeditions vary by season?

02

How do observed summit success and per-member fatality vary across team-size groups?

03

How do expedition volume, termination reasons, and altitude add context to those patterns?

02 · Preparation

From expedition records to comparable measures

The Himalayan Database records expeditions, not a controlled experiment. I prepared the fields for consistent filtering, then calculated separate measures for expeditions and individual members so their denominators would not be mixed.

  1. 01

    Scope

    Retained records from Spring, Autumn, Winter, and Summer between 1950 and 2024.

  2. 02

    Standardize

    Normalized season labels and converted year, team size, death, success, and highpoint fields for analysis.

  3. 03

    Engineer

    Created five team-size bands and grouped detailed termination reasons into readable outcome categories.

  4. 04

    Calculate

    Separated deaths per 100 expeditions, deaths per member, and expedition-level success into distinct metrics.

  5. 05

    Build

    Connected season, year, and Everest-status filters to the summary measures and seven analytical views.

  6. 06

    Qualify

    Flagged groups with fewer than 30 expeditions and added explanatory notes beside the visual evidence.

03 · Dashboard structure

A path from orientation to interpretation

I organized the dashboard in layers, so a first-time visitor can understand the scope before making comparisons and can open supporting context when a pattern needs explanation.

01 · Orient

Five summary measures

Total expeditions, member deaths, deaths per 100 expeditions, average team size, and main-peak success.

02 · Compare

Seven analytical views

Season, team size, time, termination reason, altitude, and their intersections provide different lenses.

03 · Interrogate

One shared filter state

Season, year range, and Everest status update every measure and chart together for consistent comparison.

04 · Explain

Context beside the chart

Chart notes define the unit, state the observation, and point out low-volume groups or alternative readings.

04 · Selected findings

Three patterns worth examining closely

These are descriptive results from the full 1950–2024 view. Select a lens to compare the evidence. The charts remain readable in sequence when scripting is unavailable.

Finding 01

Winter recorded the highest member-death frequency per expedition.

Winter reached 9.44 member deaths per 100 expeditions. Spring was about 7.8, Autumn about 6.5, and Summer about 1.0 in the prepared records.

How to read this

This is a frequency per 100 expeditions—not the percentage of expeditions with a death and not an individual climber’s fatality probability. Summer also contains far fewer expeditions, so its estimate deserves extra caution.

Finding 02

Observed success increased across the team-size spectrum.

Main-peak success was 48.4% among expeditions with 1–3 members and 78.7% among those with 26 or more members.

Associated pattern, not a causal effect

Per-member fatality also moved from about 1.49% in the smallest group to about 0.95% in the largest. Team size may stand in for era, route, peak choice, guides, oxygen, or logistical support; this analysis does not show that adding members causes better outcomes.

Finding 03

Larger teams operated at higher recorded highpoints.

Expeditions with 1–3 members averaged a 7,369 m highpoint. The average for teams with 26 or more members was 8,123 m—above the 8,000 m reference line.

Context matters

A highpoint is not the same as a successful summit. The pattern shows where the groups operated in this record; it does not mean team size itself produced the altitude difference.

05 · Design decisions

Designing for interpretation, not just interaction

A

Put the denominator in the interface

“Deaths per 100 expeditions” and “deaths per member” answer different questions. I kept those labels explicit in chart titles, axes, notes, and hover details.

B

Make one filter update the whole story

A shared season, year, and peak filter keeps summary cards and charts in the same analytical state, reducing accidental apples-to-oranges comparisons.

C

Pair color with text

Direct values, labels, patterns, and written chart notes carry meaning alongside color so the evidence is still understandable when color is not the primary cue.

D

Keep uncertainty visible

Small groups receive a low-sample signal, and the written interpretations use observational language instead of turning correlations into recommendations.

06 · Limitations

What this dashboard cannot tell us

No causal conclusion

These are historical, observational records. Team size, season, success, and mortality may all be related to peak, route, era, support, or expedition objective.

Recording varies

Coverage and field definitions may differ across decades. Missing or source-coded zero values can affect grouped measures, especially altitude summaries.

Aggregation hides variation

A season-wide or team-wide average can conceal major differences between peaks, routes, years, experience levels, commercial models, and weather events.

Not a safety forecast

The dashboard describes a historical dataset. It is not a predictive model and should not be used to estimate personal risk or make expedition-safety decisions.

07 · Reflection

The interface is part of the analysis

This project reinforced that a correct calculation is not enough: the denominator, comparison set, caveat, and interaction state must also be visible. Building the dashboard pushed me to treat explanatory writing and information architecture as analytical work, not decoration added after the charts.

A next iteration would model differences within comparable peaks and eras, audit missingness more explicitly, and add confidence intervals so visitors can see which apparent gaps remain uncertain—not only which bar is taller.
