Case study 02 · Statistical analysis

When do flight delays compound—and how much do they carry through?

I studied a year of New York City departures to move from broad exploratory patterns to testable questions. The result is a seven-part analysis of timing, distance, carrier differences, data quality, and the relationship between departure and arrival delay.

01 · Overview

A familiar problem, examined one claim at a time.

Flight delays affect missed connections, passenger trust, and airline operations, but a single average can hide when delays grow and whether flights recover time after takeoff. I used the nycflights13 dataset, which records every flight that departed New York City’s three major airports in 2013, to investigate those patterns.

Rather than jump directly to a model, I treated the work as a sequence: understand the distributions, test how stable the patterns are, inspect the documentation and missingness, estimate uncertainty, then evaluate focused hypotheses and a regression model.

Core question

Which observed delay patterns are large enough to matter—and which only look convincing because the dataset is large?

02 · Analysis path

The model was the last step, not the first.

Each report answered a narrower question and exposed a new decision to document before moving forward.

  1. 01

    Explore

    Mapped delay distributions, carrier volume, time-of-day patterns, and recovery after departure.

  2. 02

    Question rarity

    Separated genuinely unusual events from low-frequency carrier and route groups.

  3. 03

    Stress-test samples

    Compared repeated samples to see which patterns remained stable and which shifted for small groups.

  4. 04

    Read the data

    Audited encoded time, negative delays, missing arrivals, structural gaps, and outliers.

  5. 05

    Quantify uncertainty

    Built confidence intervals and checked whether derived variables created mechanical correlations.

  6. 06

    Test & model

    Used Welch’s t-test, a proportions test, ANOVA, and ordinary least squares regression.

03 · Key findings

Three views of how delay behaves.

Use the finding labels to compare timing, distance, and delay propagation. Every view pairs statistical evidence with its practical meaning.

Finding 01

Evening flights averaged 26 more minutes of departure delay.

Flights departing from 5:00–11:59 a.m. averaged 1.79 minutes of delay; those departing from 5:00–11:59 p.m. averaged 27.79 minutes. The difference exceeded the five-minute threshold defined in advance as practically meaningful.

Statistical read

Welch’s one-sided t-test: t = 142.09, df = 125,496, p < 2.2 × 10−16. The 95% confidence interval placed the true mean difference at no less than 25.70 minutes.

Finding 02

Short flights were slightly more likely to arrive late.

The late-arrival rate was 39.2% for flights over 1,000 miles and 41.7% for flights at or below 1,000 miles—a difference of 2.5 percentage points.

Statistical read

Two-sample proportions test: χ² = 209.23, df = 1, p < 2.2 × 10−16. The difference was statistically clear but practically modest: the 95% interval for long minus short was −2.84 to −2.16 points.

Finding 03

Departure delay explained most of the variation in arrival delay.

In 327,346 complete flight records, each additional departure-delay minute was associated with about 1.02 additional arrival-delay minutes. The simple model explained 83.7% of observed arrival-delay variation.

Statistical read

Slope = 1.0191, p < 2 × 10−16; intercept = −5.8995 minutes; residual standard error = 18.03 minutes. The strong association does not, by itself, establish that changing departure delay will cause the same change at arrival.

04 · Data care

The definition of “delay” changes the story.

Before drawing conclusions, I checked what the fields represented and what disappeared when incomplete records were removed.

  1. Encoding

    12.6 vs 34.9 minutes

    Mean departure delay was 12.6 minutes when early departures were retained as negative values, and 34.9 minutes after excluding them. Both are valid summaries of different questions.

  2. Missingness

    9,430 paired gaps

    Arrival delay and air time each had 9,430 missing values. Their matching pattern is consistent with cancelled or diverted flights, not simple random entry errors.

  3. Outliers

    More than 35 minutes late

    The 1.5 × IQR rule flags departure delays above 35 minutes, but the right-skewed tail represents real disruptions that should not be removed automatically.

Interpretive decision

Complete-case results describe flights with recorded arrival outcomes. They should not be presented as a complete measure of disruption, because cancelled or diverted flights may be absent from the modeled subset.

05 · Limitations

What these results can—and cannot—say.

Association, not causation

These are observational records. The regression describes a strong relationship; it does not prove that a one-minute intervention at departure causes exactly 1.019 minutes at arrival.

Unadjusted comparisons

Carrier, timing, and distance results do not control for route, airport, weather, aircraft, or schedule mix. Those factors may explain part of the observed differences.

Time-group definition

The morning/evening test uses actual departure time. A long delay can move a flight into a later group, so scheduled departure hour would be a useful sensitivity check.

Large-sample significance

With hundreds of thousands of records, small effects can yield tiny p-values. That is why the analysis reports effect sizes and practical thresholds alongside significance tests.
