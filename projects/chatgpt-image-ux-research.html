Case study 03 · Team UX research

How college students use ChatGPT to make images—and where it stops working for them

Students valued the speed of image generation, but struggled to make a precise change without losing something they wanted to keep. I studied that gap through interviews, think-aloud tasks, and a usability survey.

01 · Overview

A fast first draft, a difficult final edit

Image generation can turn a rough idea into something visible quickly. But when students wanted to fix one detail, the system sometimes changed other parts of the image, forgot earlier instructions, or produced text they could not use. We asked how college students use ChatGPT’s image tools and what helps or blocks them from getting an image they are satisfied with.

Research questions

RQ1

How and why do college students use ChatGPT image generation across academic, personal, and creative contexts?

RQ2

What helps or prevents students from turning an idea into a satisfactory image through ChatGPT?

02 · Study design

Listen first, then watch people edit

The team recruited adults who had used ChatGPT to generate or edit at least one image. Interviews explored their goals and workarounds. In usability sessions, participants edited the same illustration three ways while thinking aloud, then completed two tasks and the 10-item System Usability Scale (SUS).

Participants gave consent and were asked not to share private information. The study used a Figma affinity map, Qualtrics, Excel, session recordings, and notes.

08 interviews

Explore the why

15–20 minute semi-structured conversations with students aged 24–30. The team coded responses and mapped themes.

08 think-aloud tests

Observe the how

Three image-editing tasks on one shared illustration with students aged 21–34 from computer science, HCI, data science, and architecture. We tracked completion, prompts, iterations, surprises, frustration, and help.

08 SUS responses

Measure perception

After the first two tasks, participants answered the SUS in Qualtrics. The team compared the mean against a score of 70.

My contribution

I ran two interviews, coded those transcripts first using 14 codes, and scored the SUS responses for participants P03 and P04 before contributing to the full eight-person analysis. I also collaborated on the affinity map and the data, performance-metrics, and self-reported-metrics reports.

03 · Interview findings

Convenience draws people in. Control shapes what they trust.

The eight interviews produced 24 codes grouped into seven affinity-map clusters. Three themes connected the examples: AI helps people work quickly and generate ideas; most users still need to refine what it makes; and accuracy, quality, and control determine whether a result feels usable.

A

Theme 01

A convenient starting point

Students used images for research diagrams, class posters, profile pictures, room ideas, photo edits, and children’s activities. Familiarity, speed, and free access made ChatGPT easy to try.

4 participants said Gemini made better-looking images; some used both tools to compare.

B

Theme 02

Refinement is part of the work

People added references, wrote detailed prompts, rated outputs, restarted with merged instructions, or finished edits by hand in tools such as Canva.

Participants treated generated results as a first pass more often than a final image.

C

Theme 03

Small changes can cost trust

Faces and hair changed unexpectedly, image text was scrambled, and earlier directions sometimes seemed to disappear in longer chats.

Some participants stopped after a few corrections when results no longer improved.

“Using it to get 80 percent of the visual work done quickly is a huge time-saver for students. You just have to be ready to do a bit of editing yourself at the end.”

MSc data science student · Interview quote, lightly edited for readability

Where participants hesitated

Professional headshots, exact charts, and precise labels came up as poor fits. One participant avoided AI image tools entirely because of environmental concerns and a feeling that they take creating away from people.

04 · User personas

Different motivations. One shared need: stay in control.

The team turned interview patterns into four personas. Each reflects a different relationship with AI image making, while accuracy and the ability to keep ownership of the result recur across them.

05 · Think-aloud & SUS

Participants finished the tasks. Precision still took effort.

All eight participants accepted an image after each of the three tasks. Direct success was high, but two tasks required extra prompting for some participants. The observed issues were less about giving up and more about getting a specific result without unintended changes.

06 · Design opportunities

Make refinement predictable and give people more control

These opportunities follow from the study evidence. They are design directions to test, not validated interface changes.

01

Keep instructions in view

Carry forward prior constraints during a long editing session, and show which instructions are guiding the current result.

Connected to: participants retyping prompts, restarting chats, and uncertainty about whether the system remembered earlier context.

02

Let people protect what already works

Make it easier to preserve faces, text, style, lighting, and scene details while asking for one change.

Connected to: unexpected changes to faces and hair, scrambled image text, and requests implemented incorrectly.

03

Support localized edits

Give people a way to select an area, describe a change, and keep the rest of the image stable.

Connected to: frustration when a small edit changed other parts of the image.

07 · Reflection

A useful signal, with limits that matter

What I learned

A high average usability score can coexist with moments where users lose control. Pairing the SUS with observation made the gap visible: participants completed tasks, but often needed prompting or accepted tradeoffs in the result.

What I would change

I would record every interview, use the same tasks in the SUS and think-aloud sessions, and recruit undergraduates intentionally. Some interviews were captured as notes rather than recordings, so those quotes are less exact.

Sample

The interview group was small and mostly female graduate students (7 women, 1 man; ages 24–30). Findings should not be generalized to all college students.

Measure

The SUS followed two tasks while the think-aloud test covered three, which makes the results harder to compare directly.

Next study

Test precise edits, preservation of existing elements, and use of prior context with a larger, more varied group.
